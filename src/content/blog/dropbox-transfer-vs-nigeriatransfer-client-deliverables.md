## Dropbox: Built for Sync, Not Always for Transfer

Dropbox excels as a cloud storage and synchronization service. It keeps files updated across multiple devices, making it a powerful tool for teams collaborating on documents or for individuals backing up their work. When it comes to sending a file to a client, Dropbox offers a few options:

1.  **Shared Folders**: This requires the recipient to have a Dropbox account and accept an invitation. It is suitable for ongoing collaboration but overkill for a single delivery.
2.  **Shared Links**: You can generate a link to a file or folder. The recipient does not necessarily need a Dropbox account to download, but the file resides within your Dropbox storage. If you are on a free plan, your 2GB limit is shared across all your files, making it insufficient for raw video or high-resolution photo sets. Paid plans offer more space but come with their own set of challenges for Nigerian users.
3.  **Dropbox Transfer**: This dedicated feature allows you to send large files (up to 100GB on paid plans) without consuming your storage quota. It generates a link that expires, and you can add passwords or custom branding. However, it is a paid feature, and this is where the local challenges often arise.

For Nigerian professionals, the primary hurdle with paid Dropbox features often boils down to payment. Subscriptions are billed in US dollars, typically around $12 USD per month for a Professional plan. Nigerian bank cards frequently decline international transactions, or virtual card services add markups, making consistent payment a frustrating experience. The process of getting a payment through can be more time-consuming than the actual file transfer.

Another point of friction is the upload stability. If Funke's Spectranet connection drops mid-upload of her 4GB video to Dropbox, she might have to restart the entire process. This wastes valuable time and data, especially when working with large files and less stable internet infrastructure.
